Before we start: this is not a list of coding tricks. After 10 years, most of what I learned has nothing to do with syntax. It is about how you talk, how you lead, and how you spend your time. I wrote these down as short rules for myself, so some of them are blunt. 😉

## Where do the lessons come from?

I grouped the 11 lessons into four buckets. Most of them sit in the first one, which tells you something.

## How you talk

**1. Don't speak about your financial situation.** Work is work. Money talk at the desk creates comparisons you don't need.

**2. Don't blame anyone who worked on the code base before you.** You don't know their deadlines, their team size or what they were asked to do. Fix the code, skip the blame.

**3. Don't talk about anyone in a bad way.** Not in the meeting, not in the chat, not at lunch. People hear about it.

**4. Don't speak just to let others know you are here.** If you have nothing to add, you add nothing. Silence is fine.

**5. Don't speak like you are the only one who understands.** You probably are not. And even when you are right, this tone makes people stop listening.

## How you lead

**6. When you lead, listen more and act less.** The first thing a new lead wants to do is act. Listen first. Then act.

**7. Don't always say no, and don't always say yes.** A lead who says "no" to everything blocks the team. A lead who says "yes" to everything breaks the plan. Both are easy. The hard part is the answer in between.

## Your code

**8. Don't fall in love with your code.** It is a tool to solve a problem. When a review says to change it, or the requirement changes, let it go.

## Your time

**9. Managing time is more important than coding.** You can write great code and still fail, because you ran out of time.

**10. Estimating tickets is the most underrated skill.** An estimate is a promise to your team. Most of us learn to code for years and never learn to estimate.

**11. Don't attend a long meeting without an agenda.** No agenda, no goal. No goal, no way to know when the meeting is done.

## Is there a lesson that did not fit anywhere?

Yes, one more: **differentiate between your daily work routine and your hobby.** I wrote this one as a single line in my notes. The idea is that the two are not the same thing, and mixing them hurts both.

## The short version

| Topic     | Do                        | Don't                              |
| --------- | ------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Money     | Keep it private           | Talk about your finances at work   |
| Old code  | Fix it                    | Blame who wrote it                 |
| People    | Speak well or say nothing | Talk badly about them              |
| Meetings  | Ask for an agenda         | Sit in a long meeting with no goal |
| Estimates | Treat them as a promise   | Guess and hope                     |
| Your code | Let it change             | Fall in love with it               |

> Ten years in, the biggest improvement did not come from learning a new framework. It came from talking less, listening more and protecting my time.

There is no perfect rulebook for a career. These are the ones that worked for me. What would be on your list?
